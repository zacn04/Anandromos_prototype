# Anadromos: Business Plan (Draft for Review)

*Prepared from masterfile.md, EdTech Brain Storm, and the Math Academy Curriculum reference materials. This draft resolves every point of tension in favour of the masterfile pivot, as instructed. Sections that were consumer-market ideas (homeschool positioning, parent-facing pricing, ads, hardware) have been deliberately left out because they belong to the pre-pivot direction. Edit freely before this goes to design.*

## 1. The Problem

Anadromos exists to solve two linked problems, stated plainly in the masterfile:

Teachers do not know where to start when a student is underperforming. A teacher with twenty-five to thirty-five students cannot hold an accurate, current model of each student's actual knowledge gaps in their head. Pedersen et al. (2023), cited in the team's own research, found that nearly 38% of 4th graders and 49% of 8th graders are significantly below or above their nominal grade level, meaning the spread of prior knowledge inside a single classroom is enormous, and a teacher working from a single lesson plan is, for roughly four in ten students, teaching to the wrong level by default. Without a granular, per-student map of what has and has not been mastered, a teacher's intervention is a guess.

Teachers are under-resourced to act even when they do know where to start. Time, class size, and curriculum pacing pressure make individualised remediation nearly impossible at scale. The brainstorming research the team gathered adds a harder edge to this: teacher judgement is not neutral. Cited research (Hansen, UCL) shows that students treated negatively by teachers as early as age seven face compounding downstream effects: demotivation, a victim mindset, or a reputation as "just a bad student" that becomes self-fulfilling. This is not a claim that teachers are the villains; it is a claim that human bandwidth and human bias both push in the same direction, away from the students who most need a fair, evidence-based read on what they actually know. An objective, always-on diagnostic layer does not replace the teacher's judgement: it gives the teacher something more reliable to exercise judgement on.

The wider backdrop, drawn from the team's own source-gathering, is a literacy and numeracy crisis that is not evenly distributed: reporting cited in the research folder describes a third of disadvantaged white pupils in England leaving primary school unable to read properly, and NAEP data showing sustained declines in US reading scores broken out by demographic group. Underperformance is concentrated exactly where teacher time and school resources are thinnest.

The Edtech Papers folder adds harder numbers to this. UCSD placement data shows the share of incoming students below high-school math level rose roughly thirtyfold between 2020 and 2025, one in twelve freshmen now arrive lacking middle-school math, and the correlation between high-school math GPA and actual placement level is only around 0.25, meaning the grade a school reports is a weak proxy for what a student actually knows. Forty-two percent of students placed into the lowest remedial course had already taken calculus or precalculus. Harvard added a remedial algebra course in 2024. None of this is a UK/US literacy story alone; it is direct, current evidence that grades and course completion do not track mastery, which is precisely the gap Anadromos's knowledge profile is built to close. Jordan and Levine's research on socioeconomic variation in number competence adds a useful distinction worth building into the diagnostic model from day one: socioeconomic-driven gaps and a genuine mathematical learning disability (affecting an estimated 6 to 8% of students) look similar on the surface but respond to different interventions, good general instruction closes the former, only intensive, targeted support closes the latter, which argues for a tiered response rather than a single remediation path. On the question of whether underperformance is fixed or fixable, the heritability research the team gathered (Bessis's review of twin studies) puts genuine heritability of mathematical ability at roughly 30 to 50%, well below the folk assumption that talent is fixed at birth, leaving substantial room for the kind of developmental, metacognitive intervention Anadromos is built to provide.

## 2. The Vision

Anadromos is a diagnostic and instructional layer that sits between the student's actual work and the teacher, built around two proven models the team studied closely: Math Academy's mastery-based, knowledge-graph pedagogy, and Alpha School's approach of using AI as a classroom analytics tool rather than a content-delivery replacement for the teacher.

The masterfile is explicit that this is not a school-replacement product and not a parent-facing tutoring app. It is offered as a service to schools. Math Academy's contribution is the pedagogical engine: a knowledge graph, mastery-based progression, spaced repetition, and lesson/review content. Alpha School's contribution is the operating model: AI does the continuous, granular analysis of where a student's understanding breaks down, and that analysis is handed to a human teacher, who decides what to do with it. Anadromos combines the two: Math Academy's content and mastery mechanics, pushed further than Math Academy currently goes on diagnosing *why* a student got something wrong, not just *that* they got it wrong.

The name is deliberate. Anadromous fish swim upstream, against the current, to reach the place where growth happens, a fitting image for students who are swimming against difficult odds, and for a company positioning itself to help them make that trip.

## 3. Product

### 3.1 Subject priority

Mathematics is the first and primary product. Writing, English, Humanities, and Social Sciences follow as a second product line, built on a different mechanism because the underlying skill is different. This split comes directly from the masterfile and should anchor the roadmap: build and prove the maths product first, then extend to writing/humanities once the diagnostic engine and teacher-facing reporting are validated.

### 3.2 Mathematics: knowledge graph and mastery engine

The maths product is built on a knowledge graph in which topics are nodes and prerequisite relationships are edges, the same structural approach documented in the team's own research on Math Academy's pedagogy (topics from 4th grade through university, with a course simply being a region of one connected graph). A student's answer history is overlaid on the graph to compute a knowledge profile and a "frontier" (the boundary between what is known and unknown), and instruction happens at that frontier, never beyond it. Spaced repetition with trickle-down means practice on advanced topics also exercises the simpler topics they encompass, so review time compounds rather than accumulates linearly.

Anadromos's differentiation from Math Academy sits in what happens around a wrong answer, not in the graph itself:

- When a student gets something wrong, they are not just marked incorrect. Their working is broken into individual, clickable lines, and they are asked to point to the specific line where they think they went wrong, then classify why at that point: a typing or clicking slip, a silly mistake, a question that was genuinely too hard, or content they have not yet learned. A student who cannot pinpoint the error, or who genuinely got the whole approach wrong, has a standing option to say so instead of guessing at a line, which is itself a meaningful diagnostic signal rather than a dead end. This line-level classification feeds directly back into the diagnostic model and prevents the system from misreading a slip as a knowledge gap, or a knowledge gap as a slip, and it determines the shape of the remediation offered: a pinpointed line supports a short, focused re-teach of that specific step or sub-skill, while "I got it all wrong" triggers a fuller return to the underlying content.
- "Probe mode": for a line of working, the system can identify an underlying assumption, rule, or axiom the student almost certainly used but did not write down, and ask them to justify it. This is designed to surface the gap between "got the right answer" and "understands why the answer is right," which is precisely the gap Math Academy's multiple-choice review format does not close.
- This whole loop functions as a lightweight RLHF pipeline: the system proposes a diagnosis, the student either confirms, corrects, or says "I don't know," and that signal both improves the individual student's knowledge profile and, over time, improves the diagnostic model itself.
- Assessment weight sits as much on explanation as on the correct final answer, including the ability to explain why an incorrect variation of a problem is wrong. This is the mechanism by which the product tests understanding rather than pattern-matching.

The output a teacher sees is not a raw score. It is a live, per-student and per-class map of demonstrated understanding, flagged gaps, and a plain-language read on *why* a student is stuck, updated continuously rather than at test time.

Three findings from the Edtech Papers folder should shape how the diagnostic engine is actually built, not just how it is described. First, Chi's research on misconceptions distinguishes three different failure types that each need a different fix: a false belief, correctable by direct refutation; a flawed but internally consistent mental model, which needs the full model confronted rather than a single fact corrected; and an ontological category error, where the student has filed a concept under the wrong basic kind of thing entirely and needs re-teaching at the category level. The error-classification loop above should route into this taxonomy rather than treating every wrong answer as one undifferentiated "gap," and the line-level pinpointing described above is what makes that routing possible: knowing exactly where in a piece of working things went wrong is most of what is needed to tell which of the three failure types is actually in play. Second, self-reported reasons for a mistake need corroboration, not blind trust: production data from Math Academy's own platform shows students routinely claim "silly mistake" for what is actually a genuine misconception, whether consciously or not, so the RLHF confirmation loop should treat a student's own explanation as one input to be checked against their broader pattern of errors, not as ground truth on its own. Third, students who are already struggling (measured as below-average learning speed on a given topic) cannot reliably be given implicit credit for a skill just because they got a harder, encompassing problem right. Math Academy's own engineering experience is that this population needs forced, explicit review rather than the trickle-down credit that works for students progressing at a typical pace, a distinction Anadromos's mastery engine should build in rather than discover in production.

A related design risk is worth naming directly: aggregate percent-correct scores can hide exactly the weak spots a diagnostic product exists to find, because a student can pass a topic by consistently succeeding on its easier instances while quietly avoiding its hardest ones. The fix is difficulty-weighted item selection that gives a student "no place to hide" within a topic, rather than crediting mastery from an average. On the teacher-facing side, a coarser, complementary diagnostic is worth surfacing alongside the granular error data: when a student is underperforming, is the cause missing foundational knowledge, an ineffective practice method, insufficient practice volume, or low motivation. This four-cause framing gives a busy teacher a fast triage layer on top of the detailed knowledge-graph data, answering "where do I even start" directly, which is Problem 1 from the masterfile stated as plainly as the research allows.

### 3.3 Writing, English, Humanities, Social Sciences

This track is explicitly built for the teacher, not for independent student practice: the masterfile is direct that this is not meant to be another AI-generated-reading-comprehension product (the team reviewed and rejected that model when evaluating a comparable product). The mechanism is NLP applied to student writing, used to:

- Evaluate the strength of a student's argument relative to where they are in their education.
- Identify where the student's writing demonstrates a gap in understanding of some part of the topic.
- Identify where understanding is ambiguous, and generate specific follow-up questions a teacher could ask that student to test it directly.

This is aimed squarely at a specific, common teacher failure mode the team identified: the teacher who senses something is off in a piece of student writing but can't articulate what, and defaults to a lower mark with no useful feedback, a problem that scales badly with class size. The knowledge-graph structure is used far more lightly here than in maths, but a knowledge profile is still built: a running, evidence-based record of what a specific student has and has not demonstrated understanding of (e.g., that a student understands deductive reasoning in principle but has just misapplied it in this essay).

Research on AI essay-marking in the Edtech Papers folder is worth flagging as a build priority, not just a feature description: AI graders are more consistent than human markers but not necessarily more accurate than them, and the studies found that how precisely the marking criteria were specified mattered more to accuracy than which model did the marking. The engineering effort here should go into rubric and criteria design specific to each subject and level, not into buying the most expensive available model.

### 3.4 What was deliberately left out of this draft

Several ideas from the team's brainstorming are strong but belong to the pre-pivot, consumer-facing direction and contradict the masterfile's "service to schools, not a replacement for them" framing. They are noted here so the decision to exclude them is visible and reversible, not silently dropped:

- Direct-to-parent homeschool and after-school subscription products, and consumer pricing anchored to household spending comparisons.
- A freemium-plus-advertising consumer monetisation model.
- Purpose-built consumer hardware (a tablet, whiteboard, or "Her"-style voice-tutor device).
- Positioning the product as replacing the teacher's role in the classroom, or as a competitor to Math Academy/Aristotle-style consumer tutoring apps.

If any of these are wanted back in, that is a real strategic choice worth its own conversation, since it would pull the business back toward the B2C model the masterfile moved away from.

### 3.5 Validation from comparable deployments

This is not a purely theoretical bet. The Edtech Papers folder contains three concrete precedents that validate the specific shape of the product, an AI diagnostic layer that a human teacher acts on, rather than an AI that answers or teaches directly, and they are worth citing on their own terms rather than folded into general research background.

Eedi, a UK maths platform, runs a constrained AI tutor that activates only after a wrong answer, is restricted to the specific misconception the student just displayed, and requires a human tutor to approve every message before it reaches the student. That constrained design nearly doubled the tutoring impact of the unconstrained version in their own trials. Alpha School, the direct model for Anadromos's operating approach, does not give students an open chatbot at all; AI is used only to calibrate difficulty and flag misconceptions to the teacher, never to answer questions directly. And a randomised controlled trial (Wong and Qiu) found that students who were required to generate their own answer before being allowed to access AI retained their gains after the AI was removed, while students given free, unconstrained AI access saw their gains collapse once the AI was taken away. Taken together, this is the strongest available evidence for a specific, concrete product rule worth building into probe mode from the start: the AI should require an independent attempt before it unlocks any assistance, never the reverse.

## 4. Customer and User Model

Anadromos sells to schools and school districts. Within a school, the teacher is the primary user and the internal champion whose adoption determines whether the product survives; the student is the end user generating the data the teacher relies on. This is a B2B(2C) structure, not a consumer app, and every downstream decision (pricing, onboarding, support, data governance) should be built around the school as the buyer and the teacher as the person whose daily workflow has to get measurably easier, not harder.

Within that frame, the two segments worth distinguishing for go-to-market are: schools serving students who are falling behind grade-level expectations and lack the staff capacity to diagnose why (the more urgent, mission-critical case), and schools with students capable of and interested in advanced material (STEM A-levels, additional maths GCSE, AP courses) who are being held back by limited teaching or timetabling capacity rather than ability. The masterfile explicitly names both: students seen as "not intelligent enough to handle it" and students who are ready for more than the school can currently offer them.

## 5. Go-to-Market

The recommended entry point is a small number of pilot schools, sold in on the maths product only, with a named teacher champion in each school who receives close support during rollout. Success in a pilot should be measurable in terms a school leader already tracks: time-to-identify a struggling student's specific gap, and change in demonstrated mastery over a term, rather than proxy metrics like engagement minutes, which do not mean anything to a headteacher or district administrator.

Given the team's own competitive scan, there is meaningful signal that consumer AI-tutoring is a crowded, well-funded category (Math Academy, Aristotle, Rocky AI, Bloomy, Excellence Learning, Tailor-Ed, Strive, Shepherd, and others were all logged as active players, several YC-backed). Very little of that crowd is building specifically for the teacher-as-user, school-as-buyer model Anadromos is pursuing, which is the open lane the masterfile is pointing at, and the reason positioning this as a B2B diagnostic layer rather than another consumer tutor matters commercially, not just philosophically.

## 6. Business Model

Revenue should be structured as a school or district subscription, most plausibly priced per teacher seat or per enrolled student, rather than a per-family consumer price. This aligns pricing with the actual buyer and avoids re-introducing the consumer pricing logic (household discretionary-spend comparisons, freemium-plus-ads) that belongs to the earlier direction.

Cost discipline matters more here than in a typical SaaS business because the core product runs on AI inference, and the team's own research flagged that this is exactly why comparable products (their research cited Math Academy and Aristotle specifically) carry high costs. Problem 2 in the masterfile, teachers being under-resourced, only gets solved if the product itself is affordable enough for resource-constrained schools to buy, which makes cost architecture a mission-critical constraint, not just a margin question. The practical implication is a hybrid architecture: a non-AI, deterministic mastery/spaced-repetition engine (cheap, Math-Academy-style) handles the bulk of day-to-day practice, while targeted, more expensive AI calls are reserved for the moments that actually require reasoning: probe-mode questioning, error classification, and the writing/humanities NLP diagnostics. This keeps the expensive AI budget concentrated on the highest-value diagnostic moments rather than spread across every interaction. A finding worth building into the architecture decision itself: a 259-person randomised trial found that adaptively personalised problem sequencing showed no measurable advantage over simple random interleaving. That is a direct argument for not over-engineering the expensive, adaptive part of the sequencing layer; the cheap, simple version may already capture most of the value. Math Academy's own engineering history backs this up from the other direction: they tried Bayesian Knowledge Tracing first, a more sophisticated statistical model, and abandoned it for a simpler rule-based credit-flow system because the sophisticated version became unmanageably complex without a corresponding gain in accuracy. The lesson for Anadromos is to default to the simplest architecture that satisfies the pedagogy, and to treat every additional layer of adaptive machine learning as something that has to earn its cost, not something owed to the product by default.

The knowledge graph itself is a genuine, defensible moat rather than something a competitor can generate with an LLM overnight. Math Academy's own account of building theirs describes roughly 250 hours of manual expert labour encoding prerequisite and "encompassing" relationships across around 2,500 topics, done before their own tooling existed to speed it up. Anadromos should budget the equivalent expert-hours for its own graph as a real capital cost in the financial model, not a rounding error in the engineering budget, and should treat the finished graph as intellectual property worth protecting once built.

On pricing itself, the research folder offers a useful reframe: benchmark the per-seat or per-student price against what a school already pays for a human intervention, a teaching assistant or an outside interventionist, not against consumer subscription prices or free apps. Math Academy's own positioning is "26 times cheaper than a private tutor" rather than a comparison to other software; a school-facing pitch should use the equivalent comparison against the cost of the staff time the product is meant to multiply. The same research also flags a structural argument for staying paid-only and school-facing rather than chasing a free, mass-market tier: free, high-volume consumer products are commercially forced to soften their pedagogy to retain casual, unserious users, because there is no accountability mechanism until money changes hands. A paid, school-procured product does not face that pressure, which is a genuine, defensible reason (not just a mission-driven one) to hold the B2B line the masterfile sets out. One more piece of language worth carrying into sales conversations: framing the product as bringing the individualised-correction discipline that school sports and music programmes already take for granted into academic subjects, since that discipline is a familiar, non-threatening frame for a school buyer who might otherwise treat "AI diagnostic layer" as an abstraction.

## 7. Technology and Pedagogy Foundations

Two research syntheses the team produced sit directly underneath the product and are worth carrying into the design brief as-is, since they explain *why* the mechanics above work rather than just *what* they are:

**Knowledge graph pedagogy.** Mathematics is stored as a directed graph of topics connected by prerequisite and "encompassing" relationships; a student's knowledge profile is computed by overlaying their answer history on that graph, and the "frontier" (topics whose prerequisites are satisfied but which are not yet mastered) is where teaching should always be targeted. Spaced repetition trickles down the graph, so reviewing an advanced topic partially refreshes the simpler topics beneath it.

**Transfer of learning.** The working definition the team settled on: transfer is the activation and successful application of mental structure built in one context to a task in a different context, and it depends on whether that structure was encoded as an abstraction (versus a memorised ritual), how structurally similar the two contexts are, and whether the learner can recognise the match. Practically, this means every unit needs a "transfer in" (a real-world situation the student already understands, used to introduce the new maths as a formalisation of something they believe) and a "transfer out" (the student generates their own novel example of the concept operating in the world, the single strongest available test of whether real understanding, not pattern-matching, occurred). This structure should carry through directly into lesson and assessment design.

**The learning-versus-performance distinction.** Soderstrom and Bjork's research, central across the Edtech Papers folder, establishes that how well someone performs during or immediately after instruction and how much they have actually, durably learned are different things that often move in opposite directions. Conditions that feel smooth and produce good immediate performance (rereading, blocked practice, heavy scaffolding) frequently produce worse long-term retention; conditions that feel effortful and depress immediate performance (retrieval practice, spaced and interleaved practice, productive struggle) often produce better learning. This has a direct product consequence: every easy-to-measure metric a teacher dashboard could show, same-session accuracy, engagement time, completion streaks, is a performance measure, not a learning measure, and Anadromos's mastery signal has to be validated against delayed, cross-session retention rather than immediate accuracy, or the product will confidently report mastery that is not actually durable. This is not a hypothetical risk: the research folder cites adaptive mastery-tracking models in production that overpredicted next-session performance by roughly 47 to 58% because they did not correctly model forgetting.

**Where letting a student struggle first helps, and where it doesn't.** Kapur's work on productive failure found that letting students attempt a problem before any instruction, then explicitly connecting their attempts to the canonical method afterward, produces better transfer than direct instruction first, without costing procedural fluency, an argument for "try before teach" sequencing at the frontier of the knowledge graph rather than always leading with a worked example. The countervailing finding is the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga and colleagues): scaffolding and worked examples that help a novice actively hurt a student who already has some competence in a topic, because the extra structure becomes redundant friction rather than support. Both point to the same design requirement: how much guidance the system offers on a topic has to fade as a student's demonstrated mastery of that topic rises, rather than staying fixed or only ever increasing with difficulty.

**What the evidence does not support.** Discipline here matters as much for credibility with school buyers as for the product itself, so it is worth being explicit about what the research folder rules out rather than only what it supports. A meta-analysis of gamification in learning (Sailer and Homner) found a real but modest overall effect, concentrated more in engagement than in actual learning, and found specifically that competitive, comparison-based elements (leaderboards) risk demoralising lower-performing students while purely informational elements (progress indicators, points tied to correct effort) are comparatively safe; any gamification Anadromos builds should be screened against Self-Determination Theory's autonomy, competence, and relatedness framework, which also explains why extrinsic reward mechanics can crowd out and undermine a student's own motivation to engage (the overjustification effect). Growth mindset interventions, similarly, have a real but small effect (on the order of g = 0.10) concentrated specifically in at-risk and low-socioeconomic-status students, and only work when the surrounding school culture actually supports the message; it is not the universal lever popular treatment of it implies, and Anadromos should not lean on mindset messaging as a substitute for the diagnostic and instructional mechanics that do the actual work.

## 8. Oversight and Trust

Because the product sits between AI-driven diagnosis and a teacher's judgement about a real child, schools and teachers need visibility into what the AI is actually doing. Teachers and school administrators should have access to logs and transcripts of AI-student interactions, and the ability to flag or restrict what the AI engages with, the same trust mechanism the team identified as a "big win" in earlier consumer-facing thinking, reframed here for a school/teacher administrator rather than a parent. This matters commercially as well as ethically: a school will not adopt a diagnostic tool it cannot audit.

Two findings sharpen what "auditable" actually has to mean in practice. The first is a legibility requirement: Bessis's argument about mathematics being a trust system built on humans being able to check each other's reasoning applies directly here, a diagnosis the AI hands to a teacher has to be explainable in terms the teacher can independently verify, not just statistically accurate, or the teacher cannot exercise the judgement the whole system is built to support. Interpretability of the diagnostic output is a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have. The second is a real constraint on the transcript-access feature itself: research on the cognitive cost of AI oversight found that unstructured monitoring of AI output measurably degrades the overseer, in the studies cited, 39% more errors and 39% higher attrition intent among people doing unstructured AI review. Handing a teacher a raw, undifferentiated transcript log to audit at their own initiative will not get used and will add to the exact resource burden Problem 2 in the masterfile is meant to relieve. Transcripts need to be curated and triaged (flagged moments, not full logs) rather than dumped wholesale on the teacher.

## 9. Risks and Design Cautions

A few risks surfaced directly by the research are worth stating plainly rather than discovering later.

Math Academy's own public philosophy, as documented in its own blog, argues that school's only remaining value once a mastery engine exists is socialisation, and that teachers become accountability-monitors rather than instructors. Anadromos is built on Math Academy's pedagogical engine but explicitly rejects this framing; the masterfile is clear that this is a service to schools, not a case for their obsolescence. Because Anadromos's own positioning is so close to Math Academy's technically, this is a real and easy trap to fall into by inheritance rather than by choice, and marketing, sales, and product copy should be checked against it specifically, not just against the general "we support teachers" line.

The go-to-market plan should anticipate a structural objection from school buyers themselves: headcount-based school funding and standardised test-score accountability systems often structurally discourage schools from letting individual students progress off the grade-level pace the funding model assumes, even when a mastery-based tool shows a student is ready to move faster or needs to slow down. This is not a product problem to solve technically; it is a sales conversation to prepare for explicitly with pilot schools before it surfaces as a surprise objection.

Two further product risks are worth flagging for the design brief directly. Students will attempt to game any system that grants credit, and production data from a comparable platform documented specific patterns worth designing against from the outset: skipping worked examples and getting stuck, ignoring error feedback, repeatedly answering "I don't know" to skip a diagnostic rather than engage with it, and cross-referencing answers across tabs. A mastery signal that only checks final correctness is exploitable in all of these ways; weighting credit by how an answer was reached, not just whether it was correct, is the direction the research points, though it raises its own data-governance and child-privacy questions that should be resolved deliberately rather than by default. Separately, "probe mode" carries a specific failure mode worth naming: an AI asking leading questions can unconsciously cue the correct answer through its own phrasing and timing (the same effect documented in human teachers and known as the Clever Hans effect), producing what looks like demonstrated understanding but isn't. Periodic assessment with the usual conversational cues deliberately stripped out is worth building in as a check against the diagnostic engine fooling itself.

One open engineering problem is worth flagging as a genuine opportunity rather than a solved feature: Math Academy's own team describes "re-entry after a lapse", a student returning after time away facing a stale, too-difficult task queue calibrated to where they were, not where they are now, as an unsolved problem in their own system. A well-designed re-entry flow (an easier reintroduction session, explicit credit for returning) is a concrete, currently-open differentiation opportunity for Anadromos rather than something to copy from a competitor that has already solved it.

## 10. Roadmap (Suggested, for Review)

Phase 1, Maths MVP: knowledge graph and mastery engine for a bounded curriculum slice (e.g., one or two grade bands), the error-classification loop, and a teacher-facing dashboard showing per-student and per-class gaps. Piloted with a small number of design-partner schools.

Phase 2, Diagnostic depth: probe mode, explanation-weighted assessment, and the RLHF-style "why did you get this wrong" loop layered onto the Phase 1 engine.

Phase 3, Writing/Humanities: the NLP diagnostic track for teacher use, built once the maths product has validated the teacher-facing reporting model and the school sales motion.

Phase 4, Expansion: additional subjects, additional grade bands, deeper cross-subject knowledge profiles.

## 11. Open Questions for the Founders

A few decisions in the source material were left unresolved by the masterfile and should be settled before this goes to design: exact pricing mechanism (per-seat vs. per-student vs. per-district flat fee); how much of the AI stack should be built in-house versus API-based at MVP stage, given the cost sensitivity described in Section 6; which grade band and geography to pilot in first; and how deep the writing/humanities product goes in v1 versus what gets deferred to Phase 3.

## 12. Brand

The name Anadromos (from the Greek for fish that swim upstream to spawn) and the accompanying koi-fish mark position the company around the idea of swimming against a difficult current to reach a place of growth, a natural fit for a product built around students the system has, for one reason or another, been unable to properly serve.

# Changelog — Design-phase decisions (18 July 2026)

*Everything above this line is the business plan as written before prototyping. This section records the product decisions made while building the interactive prototypes (Teacher / Student / Parent POVs), so the plan reflects what was actually designed. Where a decision changes something above, it says so explicitly rather than editing the original text.*

## 1. A read-only parent view was reintroduced (revises §3.4 and §4)

§3.4 deliberately excluded all direct-to-parent products as belonging to the pre-pivot consumer direction, and §4 framed the product as strictly B2B(2C) with the teacher as primary user. During design we added a **read-only Parent POV**. This is **not** a return to the consumer model: it is not separately sold, carries no parent-facing pricing, has no ads, is not a tutoring surface, and shows **no marks, scores, or peer comparison**. It exposes only the child's mastery/understanding, upcoming lessons and homework, and struggle-points from completed sessions.

The strategic read: this stays inside the school-as-buyer, teacher-as-primary-user model — the parent view is a byproduct surface of the same knowledge profile, offered through the school, not a consumer product. It is flagged here (rather than folded in silently) because it does touch the boundary §3.4 drew, and the founders may want to decide deliberately how far a parent surface goes before it re-opens the consumer questions that section closed.

## 2. Error-classification loop is multi-select, per-line (deepens §3.2)

§3.2 describes the student pinpointing the line where they went wrong and classifying why. The prototype extends this to **multi-select**: a student flags *every* line where a technique mistake occurred, and each flagged line carries its own reason (slip / silly mistake / too hard / not yet learned, plus a free-text "Other") and its own focused re-teach of that specific step. The standing "I got it all wrong / not sure where" option is retained. This makes the routing into Chi's misconception taxonomy (§3.2) finer-grained — one piece of working can now yield several distinct, separately-classified signals rather than one.

Practice input was also specified concretely: a notation palette for the final answer, with an **optional handwriting upload** (photo of the student's working) that the teacher can see in the activity log.

## 3. Free play, gated behind lessons and feeding the profile (new mechanic)

A **Free Play** mode was added: the full maths topic map, where a student unlocks a subtopic's practice by completing its lessons, then gets unlimited AI-generated problems. Free-play work **contributes to the same mastery data and "last studied" recency** as taught practice. It is deliberately **locked behind lesson completion** so it cannot be used to route around the taught mastery path — consistent with the "no place to hide" and forced-review cautions in §3.2.

## 4. Teacher oversight flags were made concrete (implements §8)

§8 called for curated, triaged flagged moments rather than raw transcript dumps. The prototype implements three concrete flag types: **uncertain diagnosis**, **possible gaming**, and **mastery/retention mismatch** — the last directly operationalising the learning-vs-performance / forgetting-model risk raised in §7. These feed a teacher **"address in person" to-do list** on the dashboard, keeping the oversight burden curated per the cognitive-cost-of-oversight finding in §8.

## 5. Knowledge graph presented in two views (design detail on §7)

In the interface the graph appears as a **Focused** view (only the class/year "basket of topics") and an **All of Maths** view (the full graph). Node click surfaces last-worked, next-review, and retention timings, making the spaced-repetition and frontier concepts from §7 directly legible to teachers, students, and parents.

## 6. Class setup carries a per-year "basket of topics"

Beyond the roster, a class is configured with a **basket of topics for the year** that drives the Focused graph view, problem-set prerequisite gating, and the "expected pace" comparison in the student drill-down. This is the concrete data structure behind the "curriculum slice being piloted" mentioned in the MVP brief's onboarding screen.

## 7. Reason-coded routing in the review recommendation algorithm (deepens §3.2)

*Why* the student said they got the answer wrong should influence how the recommendation algorithm codes the subtopic for reviews — the self-reported reason is a routing input, not just teacher context. Examples of the intended routing:

* **Too hard / "I understood nothing"** → the student has to redo the lesson.
* **Silly mistake** → the student gets to do a similar question with different numbers.
  * If the student gets that question wrong too, the subtopic is instantly coded as **"hasn't learned properly"**.

## 8. Study-mode definitions

The four study modes referenced throughout the product, defined precisely:

* **Lesson** — students learn a subtopic and the corresponding concepts related to the subtopic. During lessons, students learn a concept and are then immediately tested on their understanding of the concept within the lesson.
* **Review** — spaced repetition of specific subtopics that contribute to a student's knowledge profile.
* **Freeplay** — repetition of specific subtopics, where students do not have to follow our knowledge-graph-based recommendation algorithm. This can contribute to a student's knowledge profile, but we will still require the student to follow our program of spaced repetition for the "compulsory" reviews.
* **Problem Set** — questions that the teacher sets on specific subtopics. Depending on what students get right or wrong, our recommendation algorithm acts accordingly. It is not yet clear how the teacher would set the homework, but possible options include:
  * Scanned and uploaded by the teacher
  * Scanned by the teacher and integrated onto the platform using AI
  * AI-generated problems audited by the teacher
